What Drives Rural Growth?: Determinants of Demographic Expansion in Rapid-Growth European Rural Areas27/02/2026IntroductionEuropean rural areas have been considered to be generally suffering from long-term population decline (Bański et al., 2020; Mascherini et al., 2023; Perpiña Castillo et al., 2023), a trend that is expected to aggravate in the following decades Curtale et al. (2025). Rural depopulation is a direct result of persistent out-migration and second-order factors, including declining fertility rates, population ageing and rising mortality (Johnson and Lichter, 2019). Out-migration of young individuals reduces birth rates and exacerbates socio-economic decline in rural areas, further widening the territorial gap with cities (Bernard et al., 2025).Even if these processes are widespread across Europe, most studies agree that the intensity of population decline differs substantially across European rural areas, often rendering a contrasting landscape of declining villages and towns located close to areas undergoing demographic expansion (Alonso et al., 2025; Bontje et al., 2012; Eliasson et al., 2015; Newsham and Rowe, 2023; Rizzo, 2016; Tóth and Kincses, 2025), with marked variations in demographic indicators and in-migration flows between rural regions (Burillo et al., 2020; Ciaschini et al., 2023; Ghio et al., 2025; Gurrutxaga, 2023; Jakobsen et al., 2025; Karpestam and Håkansson, 2021; Smith et al., 2021). Regardless of place-specific dynamics, the vast majority of European population and rural development studies are underpinned by statistics collected, processed and presented at the administrative levels—either for Local Area Units (LAU) or upper administrative regions (NUTS). This prevents a detailed understanding of demographic patterns at the local and neighbourhood levels, potentially leading to misconceptions regarding population growth dynamics in small areas (Eimermann et al., 2022; Tóth and Kincses, 2025). Moreover, the study of demographic dynamics in growing rural areas is dominated by the literature focusing on rural decline. Thus, there is relatively greater awareness regarding the factors making rural residents leave—push factors, as opposed to the mechanisms attracting new residents to rural areas—pull factors.In this study, we address these research gaps by exploring the evolution of population figures across rural Europe over the 2011-2021 period. Building on the European 1 km² population grid, we map inter-census population dynamics and produce a granular analysis of the determinants of demographic performance in rural areas by means of a set of nested multi-level logistic regression models. Our focus is on areas experiencing rapid demographic expansion, defined as grid-cells where population growth exceeds that of the administrative regions (NUTS 0-3) they are part of. Our research objectives are twofold: first, we aim at testing the relevance of known determinants of rural population performance in the European context at a granular scale; second, we aim at understanding the extent to which the directionality and strength of associations between determinants and population outcomes differ across geographies, according to unobserved contextual factors and degree of urbanisation. We hypothesise (H1) that the determinants described in rural (de)population studies may also explain the patterns found in rural areas at the grid level. Furthermore, we hypothesise (H2) that the strength of these associations will exhibit a non-monotonic relationship, varying according to geographical context.The remainder of this paper is organised as follows: Section 2 defines rural areas and characterised rural performance during the 2011-2021 inter-census period. Section 3 presents a theoretical framework informed by relevant literature on the determinants of rural demographic performance; Section 4 describes the data and methods used in the study; Sections 5 and 6 present the results and discuss them in relation to existing research, respectively; Section 7 summarises the main conclusions and considers their broader policy implications.Rural demographic growthDelineating rural areasThe most established delimitation of rural areas in the EU is the Degree of Urbanisation classification* (DEGURBA), developed under the Tercet Regulation (EU 2017/2391). This classification assigns lower administrative units to different classes within the urban-rural continuum according to population density, grid contiguity and population size (Eurostat, 2024; Eurostat, 2019). DEGURBA represents a population-centric approach, extensively utilised across various policy areas, particularly those concerning cohesion policy and rural development, for strategic delineation, target definition, and funding allocation (Cattivelli, 2024). Nevertheless, its reliance on population data has drawn criticism regarding its limited capacity to represent the multifaceted heterogeneity of rural areas, specifically concerning economic structures, labour market characteristics, and access to services (Dijkstra et al., 2019; Perpiña Castillo et al., 2024; Testori et al., 2026). Furthermore, dependence on population counts at the grid-cell level compromises comparability across international applications (Zhang et al., 2025).Acknowledging its limitations, this analysis utilises the DEGURBA delimitation of rural areas, consistent with established EU policy and governance frameworks. The analysis focuses on low-density and semi-dense rural grid cells, encompassing classes 311, 312 and 313 in the DEGURBA level 2 (2011). Class 311 includes very low-density rural grid cells, with less than 50 inhabitants per km²; Class 312 includes low-density rural grid cells, with a density of at least 50 inhabitants per km² and are not part of a rural cluster, and; Class 313 includes rural clusters, consisting of contiguous—using eight-point contiguity—grid cells with a density of at least 300 inhabitants per km² and a collective population between 500 and 4,999 (EC, 2021b). The original population grid (N = 4,595,749) was subset to exclude urban and intermediate areas (classes 130, 221, 222 and 223), as well as cells completely covered by water bodies (class 310), resulting in a final sample of size N = 77,969. Taken together, the data presented in Tables 2 to 4 suggest that: (i) the difference between expanding and declining rural areas—and between urban and rural areas—is more pronounced when characterised by population counts than by cell counts; (ii) despite the greater proportion of rural areas experiencing population decline, a significant proportion of European rural areas are undergoing rapid demographic expansion relative to their territorial contexts; and (iii) rural areas experiencing population growth between census periods were predominantly classified as very low-density rural grid cells at the DEGURBA level 2 (2011), suggesting that rural population growth does not necessarily adhere to the same concentration and agglomeration logics as urban processes.Theoretical frameworkRural depopulation, resulting from persistent and selective out-migration alongside second-order factors including declining fertility (Burillo et al., 2020; Newsham and Rowe, 2025), population ageing (Prenzel, 2021) and rising mortality (Johnson and Lichter, 2019), leads to a deterioration of social and economic resilience in population-deprived regions. This feedback loop negatively impacts regional attractiveness for residents and potential in-migrants, further accelerating depopulation decline. Modifying migration patterns and encouraging settlement by young families represents the primary strategy for reversing this trend (Vennerød and Schjøtt-Pedersen, 2023). Newcomers contribute to the sustainment of local services and infrastructure (Egea and Kreichauf, 2025).Against this framework, research has increasingly focused on the motivations driving young individuals and families to return to or remain in rural areas Stockdale et al. (2018). A key area of investigation involves the study of counter-urbanisation processes, broadly defined as the relocation of urban residents to rural areas (Bijker and Haartsen, 2012; Eimermann, 2015). Counter-urbanisation is often presented as a catalyst for rural renewal, fostering diversification and revitalisation of cultural and social life (Oliva, 2010; Pileva and Markov, 2024). However, these processes can also generate social tensions, particularly through phenomena such as rural gentrification or rurbanisation (Eliasson et al., 2015; MacVicar et al., 2016; Natanzon et al., 2010; Sijtsma et al., 2012; Smith et al., 2021). Counter-urbanisation is frequently associated with health (González-Leonardo et al., 2022; Gurrutxaga, 2021; Prenzel and Tomczyk, 2025), and economic crises (Anastasiou and Duquenne, 2020; Carlucci and Salvati, 2023; Salvati, 2019; Salvia et al., 2020).The counter-urbanisation phenomenon has coincided with an increase in international migration to Europe’s rural areas (Camarero and Sampedro, 2019; Collantes et al., 2014; Egea and Kreichauf, 2025; Munkejord, 2017). These flows are frequently driven by external push factors, including geopolitical conflicts (Lacroix et al., 2025; Papadopoulos and Fratsea, 2021; Schneider, 2022). Several European regions demonstrate successful efforts to attract young families and counteract out-migration, although reversing youth out-migration remains challenging (Alonso-Pardo et al., 2023; Schorn, 2023).Regardless of the method employed, rural revitalisation invariably entails costs. Poorly managed counter-urbanisation processes can generate land use conflicts and asset degradation, such as landscape and cultural heritage (Vasárus et al., 2018). Furthermore, counter-urbanisation may provoke conflicts of interest between newcomers and established residents (Sijtsma et al., 2012), with local inhabitants potentially perceiving urban migration as a threat to their rural lifestyles and residential expectations (Areschoug, 2024). Simultaneously, many local reception and regeneration policies reflect a “predatory inclusion” model, framing migrants as resources for economic and housing revitalisation in exploitative ways (Egea and Kreichauf, 2025; Sætermo and Kristensen, 2024).Despite underlying conflicts, rural re-population has become a primary objective of rural development strategies across Europe (Bolzoni et al., 2026), as evidenced by the European Commission’s Long-Term Vision for Rural Areas (EC, 2024). This overarching policy encompasses a cross-sectoral Action Plan designed to address a range of economic, social, and environmental challenges, ultimately aiming for “stronger, connected, resilient, and prosperous rural areas by 2040”. These goals are supported by initiatives targeting specific socio-demographic groups within rural communities, including rural women, youth, and international migrants, alongside a flagship initiative focused on revitalising areas experiencing population decline.Drivers of rural demographic performanceThis section presents an overview of key determinants for rural demographic revitalisation. A structured literature review was conducted using a sample of documents identified in the Scopus abstract and citation database. The search, performed in spring 2025 and updated in early 2026, utilised the boolean string ’rural AND (populat* OR demograph) AND (attract OR stay*)’ to identify reseach documents published in English within the Social Sciences, Humanities, Economics, and Earth and Planetary Sciences subject areas between 2020 and 2026. The search yielded 283 documents, supplemented by seminal papers and reports from international organisations, think-tanks, and research centres to provide a broader policy perspective. All materials were analysed to identify determinants of rural demographic performance, specifically regarding the retention and attraction of young families to European rural areas, alongside research on human reproductive behaviour.Decisions to remain or settle in rural areas are influenced by a combination of contextual and personal factors (Ehlert, 2026), encompassing material and emotional considerations, and their intersections (Flynn and Kay, 2017). Furthermore, determinants of migration patterns are dynamic and subject to change due to evolving internal and external circumstances (Prenzel and Tomczyk, 2025). This principle also applies to demographic variables such as birth rates, which demonstrate the complex interplay between socio-economic and geographic determinants impacting family formation (Kurek, 2026).The literature examines a range of social, economic, and territorial factors, alongside territorial capitals—encompassing natural, social, economic, and institutional endowments—that are the focus of numerous policy interventions (Loras-Gimeno et al., 2025). Drawing on this multi-dimensional framework, some studies employ the concept of the rural idyll, frequently utilised in research on counter-urbanisation processes to reflect evolving stakeholder priorities in post-industrial societies (Rovelli et al., 2020). This construct incorporates personal projections regarding quality of life in rural settings, including a preference for space, tranquility, environmental quality, and enhanced safety, fostering a sense of normalcy, familiarity, and connection to the natural environment (Elshof et al., 2017; Kuhmonen et al., 2016; van der Star and Hochstenbach, 2022). Next, we outline these diverse categories of determinants for rural demographic growth within the European context, considering their overlaps and mutual interactions.Accessibility to basic servicesRural shrinkage and relocation of the younger population to urban areas has been associated with the decline of basic services (Alonso et al., 2025; Gashi Nulleshi and Kalonaityte, 2023), potentially exacerbating ‘rural resentment’ (García del Horno, 2026). Conversely, access to basic services, especially healthcare and education, is frequently identified as a prerequisite for rural renewal (ENRD, 2022; Slätmo et al., 2022). Empirical research, such as Elshof et al. (2017), confirms the fundamental role of these services—including health, education, and retail—in meeting practical needs and supporting village revitalisation. Further studies emphasise the sustainability of rural services, noting the requirement for a critical mass of users to ensure both social and financial viability, particularly in remote rural contexts (OECD, 2025).Among the broad range of services considered essential for fulfilling basic human needs, supporting well-being, and promoting social inclusion (EMPL, 2024), four categories emerged from the reviewed literature: (i) healthcare provision (Chevillard and Mousquès, 2021; Neumeier, 2024; Pérez, 2026); (ii) education services (Lehtonen, 2021; Lovén et al., 2020; Núñez-Martínez et al., 2025); (iii) small-scale retail, including groceries and supermarkets, and retail banking (Clark et al., 2024; Elshof et al., 2017; Neumeier and Kokorsch, 2021); and (iv) transport and communication infrastructure. Other services of general interest, such as access to affordable energy or remote financial services, were not specifically addressed due to their lack of spatial constraints.Granting satisfactory access to services of general interest in rural communities depends on several factors, including: (i) the physical and/or digital delivery conditions of services, specifically regarding diversity, frequency, quality, and affordability (EMPL, 2024; Huertas and Orcao, 2025; Milcent, 2023); (ii) the digital competencies of both service users and providers (Budai et al., 2025); (iii) the capacity of rural areas to attract young professionals in service provision sectors (Fergusson and Teasdale, 2022; Gibb, 2024; Jonas-Ahrend and Tenberg, 2025; Locock et al., 2025; Maclaren et al., 2024; McIsaac, 2021), and; (iv) the availability of well-developed transport systems, including multi-modal public transport alternatives (Brisc and Bodocan, 2025; Juschten and Hössinger, 2026; Kristensen and Grunfelder, 2021), innovative mobility services (Papaioannou et al., 2023; Thao et al., 2021; Thriscutt, 2022; Tordai et al., 2025) and supporting digital infrastructures (Flipo et al., 2022; Hölzel and de Vries, 2023; Huertas and Orcao, 2025).Physical and digital infrastructures are a critical pillar for rural areas, fostering economic, creative, and socio-cultural dynamism while reducing the urban-rural digital divide (Feurich et al., 2024). Transport and broadband networks are essential not only for enabling access to basic services but also for supporting broader economic activity and innovation, including knowledge-intensive business services, creative industries, and rural entrepreneurship entrepreneurship (Bergholz et al., 2024).Employment and working conditionsRural labour markets significantly influence both migration patterns and demographic trends. High employment rates, wage growth, and the presence of creative industries stimulate in-migration, while regions lacking these characteristics exhibit slower or negative demographic trajectories. Within weak rural labour markets, qualified individuals, especially young women, are incentivised to migrate to urban areas seeking enhanced career opportunities, resulting in a disproportionate representation of young men and potential sex ratio imbalances within families (Gurrutxaga and Urkidi-Azkarraga, 2025; Leibert, 2016; Mascherini et al., 2023).Although teleworking schemes are gaining relevance for rural demographic performance (Incaltarau et al., 2024), physical proximity to services and labour markets remains a significant factor in rural renewal and counter-urbanisation (Oliva, 2010). Rural areas proximate to cities benefit from enhanced access to urban jobs. Furthermore, these areas offer residents spacious housing, proximity to natural environments, increased privacy, and the advantages associated with smaller communities, including enhanced safety, and social engagement, all while maintaining commuting range to urban employment centres. Conversely, remoteness is consistently associated with rural population decline (González-Leonardo et al., 2023). Net migration into rural areas correlates positively with the size of neighbouring local and regional centres, whereas net migration decreases with increasing commuting distance (Eliasson et al., 2015). In remote areas, labour market pull factors evolve into self-made livelihoods, encompassing entrepreneurial, pluri-active, and nature-based employment opportunities (Kuhmonen et al., 2016). Furthermore, explicit settlement policies in receiving countries, coupled with labour market prospects in remote rural regions, frequently attract newly arrived international migrants, including refugees and asylum seekers (Egea and Kreichauf, 2025; Haandrikman et al., 2024; Lacroix et al., 2025; Papadopoulos and Fratsea, 2021; Sætermo and Kristensen, 2024; Schneider, 2022). Nevertheless, immigrants contribute minimally to sub-urbanisation and ruralisation processes, with migration flows predominantly directed toward bigger cities (Lacroix et al., 2025). Moreover, longer-term residency in rural areas is often contingent upon working conditions, which are frequently less competitive than those in urban settings (Górny and Kaczmarczyk, 2018), and shaped by the intersection of material and emotional factors related to social security (Flynn and Kay, 2017). Egea and Kreichauf (2025) extend this perspective by highlighting the potential for rural revitalisation policies to exacerbate racial and class-based inequalities within rural areas if they fail to account for migrants’ structural conditions, needs, mobility aspirations, and life plans.Material living conditions and inequalitiesEconomic dynamism and the prevalence of knowledge-intensive and entrepreneurial sectors enhance the attractiveness of rural areas (ENRD, 2022). However, most rural areas confront economic, demographic, and social challenges stemming from the lack of agglomeration economies (Meijers and van der Wouw, 2019). Consequently, rural areas struggle to attract new investments (Navarro-Valverde and Özen, 2025), while tourism based on natural and historical heritage remains a crucial driver of rural development (Azevedo, 2021; Dinis, 2021; Machado et al., 2022; Sánchez-Martín et al., 2024; Tomay and Tuboly, 2023). Regions attracting significant external investment are frequently associated with ‘extractivist’ models that lack local development support and community buy-in (Eriksson et al., 2024; Garbis et al., 2024).Diversification of the economy, incorporating sectors such as industry and services alongside agriculture, coupled with business innovation and entrepreneurship, can mitigate out-migration in remote areas (Clarke, 2021). Economic diversification is further facilitated by determinants of rural entrepreneurship, including local institutional environments, transport networks, and access to local services (Aldén et al., 2026). Nevertheless, human capital remains a critical factor for rural economic success. Milijić et al. (2025) demonstrate that EU regions characterised by higher shares of highly educated labour and favourable demographic profiles exhibit greater economic resilience and competitiveness over the long term.Housing affordability represents a key economic pull factor within rural areas, attracting newcomers seeking more accessible housing options (Koreman, 2023; Kuhmonen et al., 2016; Vasárus et al., 2018). This dynamic also applies to young individuals residing in rural areas. Bjerke and Mellander (2022) demonstrate, in a study of income effects related to rural-urban migration among young Swedes, that rising housing costs in urban centres may incentivise long-term residency, particularly for those outside the highest educational strata. Anastasiou and Duquenne (2020) similarly identify spatial, demographic, social, and economic inequalities as drivers of counter-urbanisation patterns in Greece. Innocenti (2021) further emphasises the role of positive, and potentially misleading, portrayals of rural areas as refuges with lower poverty rates in attracting new residents, potentially exacerbating social exclusion and long-term inequalities. Such processes of structural exclusion are not effectively addressed through welfare benefits or economic subsidies (Serra et al., 2023).Quality of life and liveabilityQuality of life is increasingly recognised as a key factor in retaining and attracting populations within European rural areas (Diogo, 2024a; Viccaro et al., 2021). Generally, decisions to settle in rural areas are influenced by a range of factors, including housing conditions, local natural amenities, and social infrastructure (Hansen and Aner, 2017). However, selection of a specific rural area is often driven by a desire to inhabit what Haartsen and Stockdale (2018) term “enchanted rural landscapes”.Furthermore, unique cultural and natural heritage assets within rural areas may stimulate rural development and contribute to population retention and attraction (Díaz, 2025; Murin et al., 2025; San-Martín González and Soler-Vaya, 2024; Signes-Pont et al., 2022; Szymanowski and Latocha, 2021).Social trust community bonds, place attachmentIndividual biographies, family ties, broader social networks, and deep integration within local communities contribute to a sense of belonging, place attachment, and stability among rural residents (García-Arias et al., 2021; Juvonen and Romakkaniemi, 2019; van der Star and Hochstenbach, 2022). Social transparency and trust promote rural dwellers’ desire to maintain residency in their communities (Farstad and Zahl-Thanem, 2021). Frequently, individuals choose to reside in rural areas motivated by a sense of belonging and attachment to the familial, physical, and social characteristics of their place, influenced by a sense of responsibility towards others—parents, partners, and children—and psychological ownership, which shapes decision-making through either choice or obligation (Ferguson and Catney, 2023; Gashi Nulleshi and Kalonaityte, 2023; Stockdale et al., 2018). Social bonds are particularly salient among young adults and families choosing to remain or return to rural areas (Sandow and Lundholm, 2023), outcomes stemming from a complex interplay of economic, environmental, geographical, and social factors (Alexander, 2023; Ferguson and Catney, 2023). Family bonds also support business and entrepreneurial decisions within rural contexts (Munkejord, 2017). However, the influence of family and place attachment on decision-making is mediated by socio-demographic profiles, as individuals with higher levels of education demonstrate distinct career aspirations and preferences compared to those with primary and secondary education (Czibere and Loncsák, 2025; Matysiak, 2022). Furthermore, broader social bonds and ‘nearness’ are critical factors facilitating social inclusion and well-being for foreign and refugee populations in rural areas, relative to urban settings (Erlandsen and Haase Svendsen, 2023; Glorius et al., 2020; Sætermo and Kristensen, 2024). Conversely, attachment to place among resident populations can also create indirect barriers to housing revitalisation (Grüner and Gruber, 2025). Community bonds are strengthened by institutional and social factors, including social trust, reduced corruption, and the quality of democratic institutions. These elements may motivate residential choices in rural areas driven by perceptions of safety and inclusiveness. Such factors are particularly relevant for in-migrants, fostering long-term settlement (Diogo, 2024a). Proximity to decision-making spheres constitutes another salient pull factor in rural areas; specifically, the capacity of local citizens to influence political decisions is positively associated with long-term population growth (Sørensen and Svendsen, 2023).Demographic and compositional factorsDespite demographic and compositional factors remain an outcome of rural depopulation and selective-out migration, previous research emphasises the role that unbalanced population structures have in reinforcing selective-out migration and undermining fertility rates in rural areas. Previous studies have extensively documented the relative un-attractiveness of rural areas compared to urban centres, particularly for young individuals, and especially for young women facing challenges in male-dominated labour markets [Gurrutxaga and Urkidi-Azkarraga (2025);Mascherini et al. (2023);Leibert (2016);;Gashi Nulleshi and Kalonaityte (2023)]. Similarly, age-selective migration reinforces demographic ageing and the polarization of demographic structures, further reducing the appeal of rural areas among specific demographics. For example, Prenzel (2021) investigated the attractiveness of aging German regions, utilising interregional labour migration and population age structure as key indicators. Their findings demonstrated a greater likelihood of individuals migrating away from these regions, coupled with a reduced propensity to migrate towards them. Nevertheless, the prevailing focus in the reviewed literature centres on the interactions and self-reinforcing mechanisms between demographic imbalances and rural decline; evidence regarding the direct role of demographic factors in attracting population or incentivising rural inhabitants to remain in rural areas is comparatively limited. (2023)Data and methodsDependent variable and predictorsThe analysis draws on the official pan-European census population grids, produced by Member States under Eurostat coordination, for 2011 and 2021. These grids comprise a regular 1 km × 1 km lattice covering the territory of the EU, plus Norway and Switzerland, resulting in a total of 4,595,749 cells. Each cell contains an integer population count representing the resident population at the time of enumeration. Consequently, the dataset consists of paired population counts for every grid cell, facilitating direct temporal comparisons of demographic patterns at a fine spatial scale. The 2021 census further includes supplementary data for selected countries, enabling estimation of a range of demographic, compositional and socio-economic indicators.To assess population distribution changes between 2011 and 2021, we initially constructed a tidy data table linking each cell identifier to its corresponding two count observations. Subsequently, we calculated absolute count changes, percentage changes, and annualized change rates, as presented in Table 2. Descriptive metrics, including absolute differences, percent change, and summary statistics of the distribution of change rates, were computed to characterise overall trends and identify patterns at the cell level (Table 3). Furthermore, to evaluate the performance of rural cells relative to regional and national contexts, we developed a dichotomous variable, sur, indicating rural grid cells experiencing demographic expansion between 2011 and 2021 and exhibiting growth rates exceeding those of aggregated NUTS 3-0 units (see Table 4). This variable implicitly represents rural areas exceeding regional and national growth benchmarks and served as a response variable in our subsequent modelling. To address factors enabling demographic growth in rural areas, we pre-collected a total of 52 candidate predictor variables sourced from publicly available datasets, including official statistics and peer-reviewed research. Datasets lacking regular lattice structures were gridded using spatial joins with administrative data and population grids. While numerous predictors were measured across multiple time points, many original gridded variables were restricted to a single year. Computational constraints and the high-dimensional nature of the dataset precluded the creation of a true panel. Consequently, multi-year indicators were incorporated as averaged values within a cross-sectional framework, thereby introducing a temporal dimension within a single‑snapshot design. To address the nested data structure and potential intra-cluster correlation, a series of multilevel logistic regression (MLR) models were fitted. Fixed effects comprised cell-level demographics and selected contextual covariates. Results indicated that rural grid cells experiencing faster demographic expansion than their regions were strongly associated with the geographic, socio-economic, and natural characteristics commonly examined in rural population and development studies.Our findings align with prior research demonstrating that demographic performance in rural areas is frequently associated with proximity to adjacent local and regional centres and the services they provide. The negative association between elevation and travel time indicates that physical isolation impedes rapid demographic expansion. Increased travel times are interpreted as reduced access to jobs, healthcare, education, retail, and other amenities concentrated in larger agglomerations (Eliasson et al., 2015; Elshof et al., 2015). Nevertheless, our European findings nuance the role of adjacent urban area size, with the association between accessibility indicators and population growth weakening as urban nuclei expand. In the fully specified model (M3), proximity to small service centres exhibited the strongest influence, while distance to larger cities exerted a weaker effect once basic needs were met. A one-standard-deviation increase in travel time to a town with a population exceeding 5,000 inhabitants (t5k) corresponded to a 14.2% reduction in the odds of rapid demographic growth (OR = 0.803, p < 0.001). Analogous effects were observed for the nearest towns with populations exceeding 10,000 inhabitants (t10) and 50,000 inhabitants (t50), with reductions of 8.3% (OR = 0.917, p < 0.001) and 4.2% (OR = 0.958), respectively. These results remained robust across all specifications, even when controlling for other physical and geographic factors influencing accessibility, including elevation (ele), which remained a significant predictor in the fully adjusted model (OR = 0.553, p < 0.001).Our results corroborate the role of employment and working conditions in shaping demographic performance in rural Europe. Regions exhibiting lower long-term unemployment (ltu: OR = 0.872, p < 0.001) and reduced proportions of early school leavers relative to the total study-age population (elv: OR = 0.929, p < 0.001) demonstrated greater demographic growth compared to regional and national trends. Both long-term unemployment and the proportion of early school leavers were significantly associated with reduced odds of rapid demographic expansion (12.8% and 7.1%, respectively), highlighting the central role of well-functioning and dynamic labour markets in attracting and retaining residents, particularly young women and individuals with high educational attainment (Alonso-Pardo et al., 2023; Gurrutxaga and Urkidi-Azkarraga, 2025; Mascherini et al., 2023). Nevertheless, our aggregated findings diverge from some local studies, such as Elshof et al. (2017), which found no evidence that proximity to employment influenced residential mobility in the Netherlands.Material living conditions and inequalities constituted a significant dimension of the analysis, assessed through a set of socio-economic indicators reflecting regional economic profiles and household finances. The results presented a mixed picture regarding the influence of these factors on rural demographic growth. Regional economic diversification was associated with greater odds of rapid demographic growth in rural areas (div: OR = 1.07, p < 0.001), while economic specialization in advanced technology sectors (hte) and high levels of entrepreneurship (ein) did not exert an independent effect after accounting for spatial clustering structures. These findings align with previous research linking diversified local economies to population inflows (Clarke, 2021; McAreavey, 2022). The household economy was operationalized through housing property prices. Other potentially relevant indicators, such as poverty rates or household income, were excluded due to limited data availability or redundancy with other predictors. The results highlighted the substantial role of property prices in driving rapid demographic growth in rural Europe. Higher average housing prices (hsp: OR = 1.16, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with increased regional growth performance. This corroborates prior studies emphasising housing as a key factor attracting new residents to rural areas, particularly in peri-urban contexts (Bjerke and Mellander, 2022; Hofstede et al., 2023; Kuhmonen et al., 2016). Simultaneously, attractive rural areas exert increased pressure on local housing markets (Smith et al., 2021; Vogl and Micek, 2025), potentially explaining the observed association between rapid demographic growth and housing costs.The quality of life and liveability dimensions are reflected by variables related to natural endowment, environmental conditions, and overall health. Rural natural endowment is characterised by environmental variables, including the hemeroby index (hem), which measures human disturbance intensity, and an indicator of scenic beauty (scb) derived from a machine-learning model trained on the output of a German reference study (Roth et al., 2021). Both indicators were positively associated with rapid demographic growth. A one-standard-deviation increase in the hemeroby index was associated with a 23.6% increase in the odds of rapid demographic growth (OR = 1.24, p < 0.001), while the effect observed for scenic beauty in the full model reached 6.3% (OR = 1.063, p < 0.001). These findings align with previous studies exploring rural population determinants (Elshof et al., 2017; Kuhmonen et al., 2016; van der Star and Hochstenbach, 2022). Conversely, harsh climate conditions were associated with lower odds of rapid demographic growth. A higher frequency of extreme hot days with maximum temperature above 35°C (ehd) was linked with a reduced odds of rapid demographic growth (OR = 0.798, p < 0.001). The effect of the average number of frost days per year (afd) was smaller and more ambiguous (OR = 1.037, p < 0.001), with the direction of the effect altering after incorporating random effects. Other amenities, such as the presence of UNESCO heritage sites (usc), were not significantly associated with the odds of rapid demographic growth. Regarding directly observed quality-of-life metrics, the model results highlight the role of health-related factors. The effect of a multi-dimensional metric informing on potential years of life lost due to all (non-violent) causes (hyl) was inversely associated with rapid demographic growth. A one-standard-deviation increase in the indicator was associated with a 16.1% reduction in the odds of rapid demographic growth (OR = 0.839, p < 0.001). Overall, these patterns align with previous studies that highlight how greener and healthier environments are becoming increasingly attractive for young families (Díaz, 2025; Diogo, 2024b; Szymanowski and Latocha, 2021), and underscore the importance of accounting for spatial heterogeneity when modelling rural demographic dynamics.The final set of predictors examined the influence of social trust and sense of community, considering levels of mutual trust and corruption. Increased social trust (sot) was associated with a modest increase in the probability of outperforming regional growth (OR = 1.05, p < 0.001), while decreased perceived corruption (cor) corresponded with reduced odds (OR = 0.78, p < 0.001). This duality may reflect that robust social trust and effective governance enhance the attractiveness of areas for both current residents and newcomers, aligning with existing literature emphasising the role of public engagement (Sørensen and Svendsen, 2023) and social trust in rural demographic performance (Farstad and Zahl-Thanem, 2021).Despite the findings’ consistency with existing literature, several limitations warrant acknowledgement. The primary limitation pertains to the research design. Although the characterisation of fast-growing rural areas utilised in this study facilitates a contextual analysis of demographic growth processes, the metric obscures variations in demographic performance. The design also precludes the decomposition of rural demographic growth by its underlying drivers, specifically between natural demographic growth and migration flows. Predictor selection was inevitably influenced by both data quality and theoretical considerations, despite considering a comprehensive set of determinants. Demographic indicators such as sex ratios and population ageing–recognised as strongly associated with rural population growth–were excluded due to poor coverage and the assumption that balanced demographic structures primarily result from rural revitalisation processes rather than constituting a direct cause. This omission may introduce omitted-variable bias by obscuring potential feedback loops between demographic composition and population growth. Furthermore, the utilisation of a cross-sectional design, rather than a longitudinal panel, stemming from constrained data availability, introduced limitations. We attempted to mitigate temporal ambiguity by employing lagged predictors, utilising the average level of each covariate measured during the inter-census period. While this approach provides a modest approximation of pre-outcome exposure and reduces the risk of reverse causation bias, it does not fully compensate for a panel design involving repeated observations of the same geographic units over time.Conclusions and policy implicationsIn the study we characterised rural areas undergoing rapid demographic expansion during the 2011-2021 period and explored the role of well-known determinants shaping rural demographic revitalisation. Building on the population grid databases for the two most recent censuses, the analysis revealed that (16.2% of European rural grid cells exhibited growth rates exceeding regional and national averages. Specifically, areas classified as very low-density rural grid cells at the DEGURBA level 2 (2011) were disproportionately represented among those undergoing rapid demographic expansion. This finding challenges the prevailing narrative of generalised population decline in rural Europe and raises questions regarding the scope of rural revitalisation claims centred on areas proximate to urban centres.A hierarchy of increasingly complex logistic mixed-effects models was employed to investigate the determinants of rapid demographic growth in European rural areas. The findings align with existing literature on rural demographic change, revealing that rapid rural expansion is driven by a confluence of factors, including access to services, labour-market conditions, material living standards, environmental amenities, and social trust. Specifically, a pan-European analysis highlighted the significance of soft and perception-related factors, often integrated within the rural idyll construct. These included environmental quality and social trust, which proved to be as influential as material determinants such as access to services, housing, and labour markets. These results provide compelling evidence supporting our working hypotheses, which posit that the determinants of rural (de)population operate in a bi-directional and context-dependent manner. Specifically, model results indicate that the effect of these determinants varies across geographical contexts, aligning with theoretical expectations of reciprocal influences. However, further research is needed to confirm the directionality and temporal ordering of the relationships between key determinants, employing longitudinal designs that track the same regions across multiple waves and allow for the direct modelling of random slopes and time-varying covariates.Despite methodological limitations, our findings offer robust evidence supporting multi-scalar rural revitalisation policies aligned with local specificities. The range of factors influencing demographic performance in rapidly expanding rural areas, coupled with the inherent diversity of these regions, highlights the potential of combined interventions addressing both material and immaterial aspects of rural life as effective policy levers for stimulating demographic revitalisation in rural Europe. However, differential effects of key determinants across regional types and territorial scales suggest that policy interventions should be scale- and context-specific, leading to rural revitalisation strategies tailored to distinctive territorial characteristics rather than deployed as generic, top-down approaches.ReferencesAldén, L., Hammarstedt, M., Skedinger, P., 2026.
